## 2. What the next experiments should have in common

Lessons from this PR, turned into design rules:

1. **Behaviour in the model's native form.** Compare next-token probabilities. No question prompts.
2. **Read where the deciding information is already in view.** No twin structure. Pair audit before scoring.
3. **A fixed-rule baseline printed beside every figure**, and bars set against it, not against a chance-level baseline plus ten.
4. **Middle difficulty by design**, with a dial that moves accuracy.
5. **The readout is the book's:** nearest pool states shown as words in their contexts, the language counterpart of the robot mosaics. The lens reads forward, to what the model will say next. RTRM reads backward, to what the state still holds of what it was told. That is the thing to measure, because it is the thing the lens does not do.

## 3. The programme, in the order I would run it

**E1. The blind reading test on arm D.** Already agreed and nearly ready. It goes first because it tests the one claim that is RTRM's alone.

**E2. Agreement across attractors: a published failure, on a clean bed.**
- **Background:** language models are known to make agreement errors when a noun of the other number sits between subject and verb ("The keys to the cabinet is/are"); Marvin and Linzen (2018) built the standard test set, and GPT-2's agreement behaviour has been analysed causally (Finlayson et al., 2021, arXiv:2106.06087).
- **Behaviour:** P(" is") against P(" are") at the verb position. Native to GPT-2, single-token, no question.
- **Dial:** number and kind of attractors, to place accuracy between roughly 70 and 85%. Gate first: if GPT-2 small does not err often enough at any setting, stop and say so.
- **Readout:** at the last token before the verb, the reconstruction: which nouns from the sentence are recovered, and does the subject or the attractor rank higher?
- **Prediction to lock:** errors concentrate where the attractor outranks the subject. Scored as balanced accuracy against the model's own choice and against correctness, each beside the best fixed rule and beside the lens.
- **Why it could sell the method:** if a reader can look at the recreated input and say "the model is carrying *cabinet*, not *keys*", that is a diagnosis in the input's own words.

**E3. Negation: is the word still there?**
- **Background:** Kassner and Schütze (ACL 2020, arXiv:1911.03343) report that pretrained models give near-identical completions for "Birds can ___" and "Birds cannot ___". Their models were mostly masked ones, so the gate is replication on GPT-2 small.
- **Readout question:** in the state that produces the completion, is the negation word recoverable in the reconstruction? Either answer is a finding a person can read: the model dropped the word, or it kept the word and did not use it.
- **Control:** the same sentences with an irrelevant inserted word of similar frequency, so "recoverable" is measured against a base rate.

**E4. Spot the planted behaviour.** Paul's suggestion, and the largest payoff.
- **Setup:** several GPT-2 small fine-tunes, some with a planted trigger phrase that changes the output, some clean, built by Francis and blinded.
- **Task:** from RTRM reconstructions on a fixed probe set, the reader names which models were altered and, if possible, the trigger. Comparison condition: the same reader with the models' outputs only.
- **Related work to cite and distinguish:** Morris et al., "Language Model Inversion" (ICLR 2024, arXiv:2311.13647) recover prompts from next-token probabilities with a trained inverter. The RTRM version is retrieval against a pool, with nothing trained.
- **Cost:** several fine-tunes at about 2.5 hours each, so it comes after E2 and E3 have shown the reconstruction readout is worth putting in front of a reader.
